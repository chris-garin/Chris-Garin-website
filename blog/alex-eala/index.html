It Was Never About Tennis: Why Brands Want Alex Eala

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 5 min read

The short answer

Brands are not paying Alex Eala for tennis. She is a category of one: the first Southeast Asian face in a global, luxury-coded sport, with one of the most passionate fan bases on earth behind her. Through what branding calls meaning transfer, a brand that stands next to her borrows what she means to Filipinos right now: discipline, poise, and above all a feeling of possibility. Nike even bent rules it never bent for Federer or Serena, letting her wear Globe and BPI patches on its kit.

Nike and other brands are bending their own rules just to get Alex Eala as their endorser. To understand why, you have to stop looking at her the way a fan does and start looking at her the way a brand does.

Why is Alex Eala such a big deal to brands?

As Filipinos, we assume brands want her because she’s the biggest thing to ever happen to Philippine tennis. But to brands, Alex Eala isn’t just another star. She’s a gateway. Tennis is one of the most global sports on earth, and Southeast Asia has never had a face of its own in it. Until now. She’s the door into a market tennis has never been able to reach.

In positioning terms, she doesn’t compete in the crowded “good young tennis player” category. She owns “the Filipino tennis star” outright. There are dozens of top-20 players in the world. There is exactly one who unlocks a market of 115 million people plus the diaspora. Scarcity drives value.

And we’ve seen how that fan base shows up. When Manny Pacquiao fought, the whole country stopped, and Filipinos in every corner of the world held their breath with us. When his championship streak dwindled, that fire never went out. It sat there like dry summer grass, waiting for a spark. The moment we got wind of Alex beating the world’s best, those embers caught fire. We didn’t wait for a Grand Slam or a World No. 1 ranking. One big run was all it took, and suddenly a whole nation was watching tennis.

Psychologists call this BIRGing: basking in reflected glory. When “our” person wins, we absorb the win into our own identity. We beat Swiatek. Filipinos do this more intensely than most cultures; think Pacquiao, think Catriona Gray. A brand standing next to Eala isn’t buying an ad. It’s buying a seat inside the national celebration.

Then there’s the diaspora multiplier. Filipinos are everywhere, and they show up. At the US Open she drew packed crowds and turned routine practice sessions into events. One athlete activates fans in Manila, Dubai, Toronto, London, and LA at once. For a global brand, that’s gold.

Why do luxury brands love tennis so much?

Luxury brands have always loved tennis, because it attracts the exact people they want: wealthy, educated, and global. It’s one of the few sports that still feels like old money. All-white dress codes. Silence during play. Royalty in the stands at Wimbledon. That’s why Rolex keeps time at Wimbledon and Mercedes-Benz backs the entire women’s tour. Luxury brands don’t buy attention. They buy association, and no sport carries more class than tennis.

But in the Philippines, tennis was always niche. So now put it together: luxury’s favorite sport, the eyes and hearts of an entire country and an entire region behind her, and at the center of it all, a 21-year-old who’s sweet, composed, confident, and eloquent every time she speaks. For brands, that’s a lethal combination that’s impossible to ignore.

It also fits where luxury is heading. Luxury marketing increasingly avoids loud, flashy celebrities in favor of understated, earned status, and Eala reads as refined excellence rather than hype. She trained at the Rafa Nadal Academy, and Nadal himself wrote about her game, maturity, and poise for her TIME100 Next listing. For luxury brands worried about an aging customer base, a 21-year-old with a huge young following is also a direct line to Gen Z.

She’s low-risk, too. Endorsements work best when the celebrity’s image naturally fits the product, and brands pay a premium for safety. Eala is scandal-free, family-backed, and has been a public figure since childhood: Globe signed her as an ambassador when she was just 8 years old.

What rule did Nike actually bend for her?

Here’s the costly signal that tells you how much leverage she has. Most Nike players wear no sponsor patches on their clothing, because Nike pays them enough to keep its canvas clean. Federer, Nadal, and Serena never wore patches during their Nike years; commentator Rennae Stubbs pointed this out at the 2026 US Open. Yet Eala’s US Open outfit featured BPI and Globe Telecom patches, rare permissions Nike reportedly never granted its biggest legends. Her US Open outfit also reportedly sold out faster than any other Nike athlete’s.

Protecting its canvas is core to Nike’s brand power. Bending that rule for her tells the market she has unusual leverage, and that signal itself raises her value to every other brand watching. The local deals back it up: Globe and BPI signed new four-year agreements at the start of 2026 guaranteeing more than $1 million annually just for kit patches. And she’s still rising, with her fourth semifinal of 2026 just reached and a Top 15 debut within reach. Brands treat young athletes like growth stocks: lock in now, before the price explodes.

What are brands really buying?

When brands choose an ambassador, they ask two questions. When people see her, what do they feel? And what qualities do they see in her? Because whatever people feel and see in her, they’ll start to feel and see in the brand. In branding, it’s called meaning transfer.

You see, the bags, shoes, clothes, and cars we buy are signals. Features matter, but mostly they’re statements of who we are and who we’re working to become. They’re votes for the person we want to be. A product can’t signal anything on its own, though. It needs meaning, and brands can spend decades building it, or borrow it overnight from someone who already has it.

So what does Alex mean to Filipinos right now? “Pinoy pride” is the surface-level answer. The deeper one: for the longest time, when we watched greatness happen on the world stage, it felt like something foreigners did. Something that happens somewhere far away, out of reach. But when a Filipino makes it there, the illusion shatters. It makes us ask: wait, so it’s possible? We can do that too?

This isn’t just a tennis thing. Every one of us has a dream we stopped saying out loud, one we talked ourselves out of because it felt too big, too far, too foreign. Every time Alex overcomes an opponent, that dream gets a chance to stop feeling like a fantasy and start feeling like a plan.

That’s what brands are really buying. Not a tennis player. Not a ranking. A feeling of possibility, for a hundred million Filipinos. When a brand puts its name next to Alex, it isn’t only borrowing her fame. It’s borrowing that feeling. And there’s nothing more valuable a brand could ask for.

Brands can spend decades building that meaning themselves, or borrow it overnight from someone who already carries it.
