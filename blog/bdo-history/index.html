Would SM Exist Without BDO? How Henry Sy Bought a Bank

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 5 min read

The short answer

BDO did not start as BDO. It started as Acme Savings Bank, a tiny thrift bank with two branches, founded in 1968. In November 1976, Henry Sy’s SM Group bought it for about ₱5 million (roughly ₱200 million today) and renamed it Banco de Oro, the bank of gold. Sy wasn’t really buying a bank; he was buying a banking license, so that the retail empire he was about to build would never depend on someone else’s money. Economists call the risk he dodged the hold-up problem.

What happens if SM needs to expand, and a bank refuses to lend it money? Well, SM made sure it would never get to this point. And so BDO was born.

Why did Henry Sy want his own bank?

In 1976, there were only a couple of ShoeMart department stores. But Henry Sy knew he was just getting started. He foresaw that this wasn’t going to be merely a “good business.” This was going to be the biggest retail juggernaut the country had ever seen.

Even then, he could see the massive amount of cash his stores were handling every single day, and that was with just a couple of stores. Now imagine full-blown malls, hundreds of tenants, millions of shoppers every week. Just imagine the volume. And for every mall he wanted to build, he’d need a lot of money, which meant financing from another bank.

That was a problem waiting to happen, because it meant someone else would have power over SM. Not just over whether it could grow, but over its money, its terms, and ultimately, its future. Traditional banks had already hesitated to partner with him or adapt to the pace of his retail expansion.

What is the hold-up problem?

Economists have a name for this exact risk. It’s called the hold-up problem: it happens when you depend on one outside party for something your business can’t live without, after you’ve already committed to a path. And here’s the catch: the bigger you grow, the more you need them, and the more power they have over you. Tighter terms, higher rates, or just a “no” at the worst possible moment.

The classic way out is backward integration: bring the critical piece in-house. Businesses even have a term for owning your own financing arm, captive finance; Toyota Financial Services and GE Capital are the textbook examples. Henry Sy’s logic was simpler: if it makes our operations run faster and more conveniently, and if depending on a bank is the risk, then why don’t we just try to own the bank.

Why did Sy buy Acme Savings Bank?

In the 1970s, wanting a bank and actually getting one were two very different things. This was the Martial Law era, and the Central Bank rarely handed out brand-new banking licenses. If you wanted into banking, you had to buy a bank that already existed.

Which is exactly why Acme Savings Bank was perfect. Founded on January 2, 1968, it was small, just two branches, with very little bad debt on its books. In November 1976, the SM Group bought it for about ₱5 million, roughly ₱200 million in today’s money, during a volatile period when other investors were hesitant to take risks. SM wasn’t really buying a bank. It was buying a license.

Sy renamed it Banco de Oro. Literally, the bank of gold. A bold name for such a small bank. But maybe he wasn’t naming it for what it was. He was naming it for what it would become. And sure enough, the malls came, the tenants came, the shoppers came, and all that cash flowed into his own bank instead of someone else’s.

How does BDO power the SM malls?

Owning BDO was never just about collecting interest on loans. The bank became the financial plumbing of the whole SM machine.

It keeps the cash inside the mall. Every day, thousands of tenants and SM’s own stores rake in millions in physical cash. Moving that across the city to an outside bank would mean armored cars, security risks, and delays. So SM put BDO branches and secure deposit dropboxes right inside the mall. A tenant can walk their daily earnings to a BDO branch down the hall after closing time. The cash never has to leave the property.

It keeps the fees inside the family. Every card tap or QR scan at a cashier costs the store a processing fee to whichever bank runs the terminal. With BDO providing the point-of-sale terminals for SM cashiers and most tenants, those fees stay within the SM corporate family instead of leaking to a competitor.

It keeps the shoppers inside the ecosystem. Co-branded cards like the BDO ShopMore card offer points, discounts, and freebies, but only for spending at SM stores and partners. The bank provides the credit, the consumer buys from an SM store, and the bank collects the interest. The money never exits the SM umbrella.

It even changed banking hours. When Sy wanted banks open until 7 PM and on weekends to match mall hours, traditional banks refused; it blew out their labor costs. Because SM owned BDO, it could simply order the bank onto “mall time.” That turned branches into foot-traffic magnets: people came to SM on weekends to do their banking, then stayed to watch a movie or buy groceries.

And it finances the growth loop. BDO gives SM a deep, loyal pool of capital for new malls, and it can lend to a reliable tenant that wants to open branches in 50 upcoming SM malls. The loan guarantees the mall will have occupied stores.

Are SM tenants forced to use BDO?

No. Tenants are independent businesses, free to bank with BPI, Metrobank, Security Bank, or anyone else, and free to sweep their profits out of the mall every night. Forcing them to route everything through BDO would cross into antitrust territory.

What SM built instead is structural convenience. Rent is typically paid into dedicated BDO accounts set up at lease signing. BDO terminals are the default offered during onboarding, seamlessly integrated into SM’s infrastructure. And for a store manager holding a bag of cash at 9 PM, the BDO depository down the hall beats hiring an armored vehicle. Choosing not to use BDO introduces friction, extra cost, and headaches. Most tenants simply take the path of least resistance.

That’s the real lesson of BDO’s origin. Henry Sy didn’t wait for a bank to hold power over his future. For ₱5 million, he removed the hold-up risk entirely, and turned what could have been SM’s biggest dependency into one of its biggest businesses.

BDO history FAQ

Who owns BDO?

BDO Unibank belongs to the SM Group, the conglomerate built by Henry Sy Sr. It was not built from scratch: SM acquired a small thrift bank called Acme Savings Bank in November 1976 and renamed it Banco de Oro.

How much did Henry Sy pay for the bank that became BDO?

About ₱5 million in November 1976, roughly ₱200 million in today's money. Acme Savings Bank was small, had very little bad debt, and came with the thing that mattered most in the Martial Law era: an existing banking license, since the Central Bank rarely issued new ones.

Why is it called Banco de Oro?

Banco de Oro literally means "the bank of gold," a bold name for a two-branch thrift bank. Henry Sy arguably named it not for what it was but for what it would become, once his malls, tenants, and millions of shoppers started pouring their cash into it.

Are SM tenants required to use BDO?

No. Tenants are independent businesses that can bank anywhere, and forcing them would raise antitrust problems. SM relies on structural convenience instead: in-mall BDO branches and deposit boxes, BDO as the default terminal provider at onboarding, and rent collected through dedicated BDO accounts. Avoiding BDO simply adds friction, so most tenants do not.
