C2 Green Tea: How URC Beat Coke and Nestea With Pure Positioning

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 6 min read

The short answer

C2 won the Philippine beverage market with positioning, not health. URC launched it in 2004 against the advice of its own marketing team, sweetened it for the Filipino palate, and anchored it against soft drinks instead of tea. Next to a Coke, a sugary tea looks like the smart choice, even though C2 has about as much sugar as the Coke. Nestlé scrambled, Coca-Cola launched Real Leaf to kill it, and Real Leaf is the one that quietly died.

Sometimes you don’t need a healthy product to look like one. The story of C2 might be the single best example in Philippine business of the power of positioning.

The bet nobody at URC wanted

In the early 2000s, John Gokongwei Jr., the founder of URC, noticed something in Japan and China: cold tea in plastic bottles, and people grabbing it from the fridge instead of brewing it at home. He wanted it replicated in the Philippines.

On paper, it looked like a terrible idea, and his own people told him so. Filipinos weren’t tea drinkers. Plain green tea is bitter and earthy, exactly the taste profile the sugar-loving Filipino consumer historically avoided. Sure, we were getting more health conscious, but nobody was trading sweet drinks for ampalaya juice. We didn’t want super healthy. We just wanted something health-ier.

Gokongwei overruled the objections, ordered the production machinery anyway, and set a hard deadline to launch. Which left his team with a product problem: how do you sell tea to a country that doesn’t drink it?

Step one: make it taste Filipino

The answer was sugar. Lots of it. URC’s flavor team tuned C2 toward the sweetness Filipinos already loved in street-side palamig, and away from the pure, earthy taste actual tea drinkers prize.

But that raises the obvious question: why make a tea product at all, only to load it with sugar? Doesn’t that ruin the whole point?

Not if you’re smart about it. Think about a salad drowned in ranch dressing. Is it actually healthy? Not really. But it still feels healthy, because it’s a salad. That’s called the halo effect: one good thing about a product makes us assume the rest is good too. C2 is still tea, so it gets to borrow everything we already believe about tea. Natural. Clean. Good for you.

Step two: pick the right enemy

The halo only works if you pick the right comparison, and this is where C2’s positioning was genuinely brilliant. There were three categories it could have anchored itself to.

Anchored to plain tea, C2 would be the worst product on the shelf. It’s loaded with sugar and buries the leaf.

Anchored to iced tea, the category Nestea owned, C2 would be just another iced tea brand, and it’s hard to lead a category someone else already owns. Nestlé dominated Philippine iced tea in 2004, but here’s the detail that mattered: Nestea’s empire was powdered iced tea. Something you stirred into a pitcher at home.

Anchored to soft drinks, everything clicked. Next to a Coke, C2 stopped looking like a sugary tea and started looking like the smart choice. Same sweetness as a soda, but it’s tea, so it feels like you’re doing something good for yourself. C2 stopped competing with tea and started stealing from soda, in a ready-to-drink bottle that bypassed Nestea’s powdered kingdom entirely.

The crazy part? C2 has about as much sugar as a Coke. Some flavors, even more. Put green tea next to a dark, fizzy cola, pair it with “Cool and Clean,” “made with 100% natural tea,” and “antioxidants,” add some leafy imagery, and our brains fill in the rest.

The giants strike back, and miss

C2 took off almost overnight, pulling a massive share of the iced tea market away from Nestea. The multinationals didn’t take it sitting down. Nestlé scrambled to launch its own bottled version, Nestea in a Bottle. And in 2009, Coca-Cola entered the ring with Real Leaf, backed by heavy advertising and a direct shot at C2: theirs was hyped as 100% real brewed green tea leaves. By 2012 they were even extending the line with Real Leaf Frutcy to chase teenagers.

Real Leaf was eventually discontinued and quietly phased out of the Philippine market. Two things killed it. First, its core flavors leaned on zero-sugar formulas to look healthier, which made it less sweet, and less sweet loses with the broad Filipino consumer. Coca-Cola, of all companies, under-sugared a drink. Second, by the time Real Leaf arrived, C2 wasn’t just a product anymore, it was the original. Consumers didn’t want a clone of C2 from Coke. They wanted C2, which was also cheaper. Real Leaf never shook its reputation as an imitation, and Coke eventually pulled the plug.

Sit with the irony for a second: the challenger that was more honest about being healthy lost to the incumbent that only felt healthy. That’s how strong the anchor was.

What C2 actually teaches

Not healthy. Not even healthier. It just felt healthier, and making us feel that way was the whole strategy that got C2 to the top.

The transferable lesson is category anchoring. Your product’s perceived value isn’t set by what’s in it; it’s set by what you place it next to. The same bottle is the worst tea on the shelf, a me-too iced tea, or the smart alternative to soda, depending entirely on which comparison you teach the customer to make. URC chose the enemy that made its weakness (sugar) invisible and its halo (tea) decisive. That’s positioning doing work that no reformulation ever could.

C2 Green Tea FAQ

Is C2 green tea actually healthy?

Regular C2 contains roughly as much sugar as a soft drink of the same size, and some flavors contain more. It carries tea’s healthy image, which is the halo effect at work, but a sweetened tea is closer to a soda than to brewed green tea. If you want the health benefits of green tea, unsweetened tea is the honest version.

Who makes C2?

C2 is made by Universal Robina Corporation (URC), the food and beverage giant founded by John Gokongwei Jr. He pushed the product into development after seeing bottled ready-to-drink tea booming in Japan and China, over the objections of his own marketing team.

When did C2 launch in the Philippines?

C2 launched in 2004, at a time when Nestlé’s Nestea dominated Philippine iced tea through powdered mixes. C2 bypassed powder entirely with a cheap, convenient ready-to-drink bottle and took a huge share of the market almost overnight.

What happened to Coca-Cola’s Real Leaf?

Coca-Cola launched Real Leaf in 2009 to challenge C2, promoting 100% real brewed green tea leaves, and extended the line with Real Leaf Frutcy in 2012. It was eventually discontinued and phased out of the Philippine market: its zero-sugar-leaning formulas tasted less sweet than the mass market wanted, and consumers saw it as an imitation of the cheaper original.
