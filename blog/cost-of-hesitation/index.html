The Brutal Cost of Playing It Safe: Lessons from “I’m Thinking of Ending Things”

Chris Garin • September 13, 2026 • 10 min read

The first time I watched I’m Thinking of Ending Things in 2020, I hated the ending.

Reading what people were saying about the film gave me the impression it was going to be a “mindfuck” kind of film, and I love those, so I went in totally blind to get the maximum shock factor.

But when the ending came, it just didn’t land for me. It felt like this long, careful buildup that never gave me the payoff I was waiting for, and I chalked it up as a pretty but confusing mess and moved on.

Then, here we are, six years later, and YouTube’s algorithm drops a video in my feed of a guy doing a nearly two-hour analysis of the film, and it hit me that I’d completely missed the point the first time.

What I’d written off as a puzzle with no solution was actually one of the saddest, simplest stories I’d ever seen.

Here’s what I took away from it.

1. The Cost of Inaction

The entire tragedy traces back to one small moment decades earlier. A young man is at a trivia night in a pub with his friends.

Across the room, he notices a woman, and for a moment the whole night narrows down to her. He feels that spark, the small electric pull that says go talk to her.

But he waits for the right opening, the perfect line, the moment when it won’t feel awkward. So instead of walking over, he just looks.

He keeps glancing at her across the bar, working up a nerve that never comes, and the longer it goes on the worse it gets, because now he’s not a stranger who might say hello, he’s the guy who kept staring and never said a word.

Then she gathers her things and leaves, and that’s the end of it.

In the only version of this night that survives, she barely registers him at all: not a missed connection, not the one who got away, just a vaguely creepy man who watched her from across a room decades ago.

He never even became a person to her. That’s how completely his inaction erased him.

He chose safety over a tiny social risk.

And really, what stopped him was the fear of being rejected: the dread of walking over, saying something, and being met with a blank look, a polite brush-off, or worst of all, laughter.

In that moment, staying silent felt like the safe choice, the way to protect himself from a few seconds of humiliation. But that one hesitation, tiny as it felt, didn’t stay small.

A life isn’t shaped by one choice; it’s shaped by thousands of them.

String enough of those small “not right now” moments together, one skipped conversation, one unsent message, one room you never crossed, and they quietly bend your entire trajectory.

Each one seems harmless on its own, but together they add up to a whole life, the specific narrow life you can only end up with if you keep choosing not to take the risk.

For Jake, it metastasized across the decades into profound isolation, until the only relationship he had left was one he invented entirely in his head.

This is the film’s real gut-punch, hidden in plain sight: the girlfriend we follow the whole movie was never a real person.

Her name, her job, her clothes, even her face keep shifting because she isn’t real, she’s a projection Jake assembled from women he glimpsed and never approached, stitched together with other people’s art, other people’s words, other people’s lives.

The one great love of his life is a composite he built alone in the dark, decades after the trivia-night woman walked out the door.

The lesson lands like a slap: the sting of possible rejection is microscopic next to the lifelong weight of regret.

Honestly, that’s the part that really got under my skin.

Watching an old Jake just sitting there, alone and full of regret over a life he never reached for, I couldn’t help but think about my own hesitations, all the times I’ve let fear quietly talk me out of doing something.

A message I didn’t send. A conversation I talked myself out of.

A girl I couldn’t take my eyes off but never worked up the nerve to talk to. An opportunity I circled but never took, telling myself there’d be a better moment.

None of those felt like much in the moment, exactly like Jake’s night at the bar didn’t feel like much.

But that’s the whole terrifying point: you never notice the life-defining choices while you’re making them. But in hindsight, they turn out to be the exact forks where a whole life quietly took its shape.

2. No One Is Coming to Save You

In the film, Jake spends decades waiting for life to get better on its own.

And by the time we see him as an old man, he’s still doing it, still sitting and waiting, as if the change he kept expecting might finally show up if he just holds out a little longer.

He hears all the motivational lines about how it’s never too late, how things turn around, how there’s a plan for you, and he’s decided they’re lies.

And you can understand why he’s bitter. But that bitterness becomes its own trap, because he uses the emptiness of the clichés as permission to keep doing nothing.

That’s the cruel twist of it. He’s not wrong that hope alone is hollow.

He’s wrong to conclude that the answer is to stop trying. Hope without action rots.

Waiting for change to arrive from the outside is the disease, not the cure, and the only thing that ever actually shifts a life is the person deciding to move first.

And that’s the realization I walked away with.

Unless I’m genuinely content with where my life is, I can’t just sit around waiting for it to get better on its own, because it won’t.

Time passes whether I act or not; the only question is whether I have anything to show for it when it does. Comfort is a quiet thing to drown in.

If I want something different, the responsibility is mine. I have to take the action.

I have to take the risks and actually make it happen, because no one is coming to do it for me, and the waiting only ever ends one way.

And if I’m not willing to do anything about it, then I’ve given up my right to complain about where I end up. The whole thing is on me either way.

3. The Slow Comfort of Giving Up

The film ends where it began: on a lone truck sitting in the parking lot of the school where Jake spent his working life.

Everything we watched, the girlfriend, the drive, the dinner with his parents, was happening inside his head as he sat in that truck in the middle of a blizzard, slowly freezing to death.

The whole film is the last performance of a mind shutting down in the cold.

What makes it unbearable is a real detail the story leans on: paradoxical undressing. Here’s how it works.

As you freeze, your body pulls blood away from your skin to protect your heart and keep your core warm. But near the end, that system breaks.

All that blood rushes back to the surface at once, and suddenly you feel boiling hot even though you’re freezing.

So you rip off your clothes to cool down, which just makes you freeze even faster. As strange as it sounds, that’s what people commonly do in the final stages of freezing to death.

What struck me watching it was how perfectly this maps onto what Jake did with his whole life.

Retreating inward felt like warmth. Staying home, living inside his own head, it all felt like protection, when it was the very thing killing him.

That’s what makes it so insidious: the freeze doesn’t announce itself as danger.

It arrives disguised as relief, the smart and cozy alternative to the cold hassle of putting yourself out there, right up until the end.

The unsettling part is how ordinary this looks from the outside. It isn’t dramatic.

It’s a man slowly choosing comfort over connection, over and over, each choice reasonable on its own, until one day the sum of them is a life spent entirely alone.

The cold never feels like cold. That’s exactly why it wins.

4. The Comfort of Expecting the Worst

Connection can be painful. People can be painful.

The film never pretends otherwise, and neither should you.

But the pig’s strange monologue and the awkwardness at the ice cream stand keep circling the same idea: the assumption that everyone is out to hurt you is far more dangerous than any actual person.

That assumption is what leads Jake to the cold, empty truck.

Treating cynicism as armor feels smart and safe, a way to never be let down. But all it really does is guarantee the loneliness you were trying to defend yourself against.

You can’t wall yourself off from pain without also walling yourself off from everyone who could have made your life worth living.

Thankfully, I’m well past believing this myself, but I’d be lying if I said it never creeps back in.

It still shows up now and then, that quiet urge to expect the worst so I don’t have to risk anything. And I’ve met plenty of people who live there permanently.

What’s striking is that they genuinely think it makes them the wise one.

They forecast that the plan will fail, that the person will flake, that the opportunity is a scam, and they wear that pessimism like insight, as if predicting disaster is the same as understanding the world.

But nine times out of ten, the “wisdom” is just a pre-written excuse.

It’s a way to talk yourself out of the risk before you ever have to face it, and then call your cowardice foresight.

I try to catch myself whenever I hear that voice, because I know exactly where the road it’s on ends.

If there’s something to take from this, it’s to get honest about the difference between caution and cowardice, because they sound identical from the inside.

Real wisdom weighs a risk and then does something about it; it plans, prepares, and moves anyway. Cynicism just uses the risk as a reason to never move at all.

So the next time you catch yourself confidently predicting why something won’t work, ask whether you’re actually protecting yourself or just building a comfortable excuse.

Assume the best often enough to give life a chance to surprise you, because a life spent avoiding every disappointment is its own slow disaster, and it’s the one that actually comes true.

Say Hello While You Still Can

The film isn’t telling you to be reckless. It’s telling you to show up.

Ask the question. Say the word.

Cross the room. Because the alternative was never safety.

The alternative is a man freezing in a parking lot with a head full of scripts he never performed, mourning a life he was too afraid to reach for.

I’ll admit I felt a little embarrassed that it took a two-hour breakdown for the message to click.

But the guy in the video is generous about it. He doesn’t blame anyone for missing it, because it’s such a dense, slippery film, and he’s honest that he only really got it himself after going back and reading the book.

That made me feel better.

Some films don’t hand you the meaning; you have to go dig for it. And this one was worth the dig.

So take the shot. Send the message, ask the question, cross the room, start the thing you keep circling.

Not because it’s guaranteed to work, but because the version of you that never tries is the one who ends up alone in the truck, warming himself on a life that only ever happened in his head.

The cold is patient. It waits for the people who keep telling themselves there’ll be a better time.

There won’t be.

There’s only now, and the room you’re standing in, and whoever is across it. Say hello while you still can.
