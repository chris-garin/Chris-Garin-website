GCash vs Maya: Who’s Actually Winning the Fintech Wars?

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 6 min read

The short answer

On scale, GCash wins by a landslide: over 94 million accounts, ₱17 trillion in payments processed in 2025, and ₱17 billion in net income against Maya’s ₱1.7 billion. But they’re playing two different games. GCash wins the attention game: users and habits. Maya plays the margin game: a real digital banking license, ₱68 billion in deposits it can lend out itself, and the payment terminals behind McDonald’s, Starbucks, Jollibee, and Mercury Drug. When you pay with GCash at those counters, Maya often earns too.

GCash just went public and is now worth around ₱442 billion. So does that mean Maya has lost? Who’s winning the fintech wars now?

I made a short video about this too, if you’d rather watch the quick version first.

Who has more users, GCash or Maya?

In terms of users, GCash wipes the floor with Maya: over 94 million registered accounts and 41.5 million monthly active users, versus Maya’s 10.7 million bank customers. Even PLDT chairman Manny Pangilinan has publicly admitted Maya is “way behind” in the wallet popularity race; “GCash” has literally become the generic word for mobile payments in the country.

The volume gap is just as stark. In 2025 alone, GCash processed a staggering ₱17 trillion in payment transactions. Maya processes over ₱1 trillion in merchant payments, which makes it a top business acquirer, but a much smaller consumer platform.

Who makes more money?

In 2025, GCash parent Mynt made a record ₱17.25 billion in net income on ₱79.8 billion in revenue. Maya booked its first ever full year of profit: ₱1.7 billion. That’s a real milestone for Maya, but it’s about one tenth of what GCash made.

So on scale, on users, on profit, it’s a landslide. GCash wins. But here’s where it gets interesting. We compare the two because they look the same: an app you use to scan a QR and pay. Underneath, they’re very different businesses, and Maya’s advantages are mostly invisible to us.

First, Maya is making more out of fewer users. GCash’s profit is built on sheer volume: millions of Filipinos paying merchants, buying load, and sending small transfers every single day, with all those little fees adding up to just under ₱80 billion in revenue. Maya’s profit is built on something else: banking. Maya holds a Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas digital banking license, so when you keep your savings in Maya, it can lend that money out directly at high interest rates. GCash can’t do that on its own. When you use GSave, your money sits with its partner bank, CIMB, so GCash has to split that revenue. Maya keeps the whole spread. With around ₱68 billion in deposits, it earns a fat, double-digit net interest margin, and it has disbursed ₱256 billion in loans since 2022.

What is Maya’s hidden advantage?

Maya’s biggest advantage is that it owns the backend. You know the pipes behind your walls? You never see them, but every drop of water goes through them. That’s Maya. When you pay at McDonald’s, Starbucks, Jollibee, or Mercury Drug, your payment often flows through a Maya machine, whether you use a card, a QR code, Apple Pay, or even GCash. So when GCash earns, Maya earns too.

That’s the “customer, not competitor” reality of the merchant space. When Apple Pay or Google Pay expands tap-to-pay across Philippine stores, Maya processes the backend transactions. A Maya terminal natively handles Visa, Mastercard, JCB, American Express, local BancNet debit cards, dynamic QR Ph codes, GCash, and ShopeePay, all reconciled into a single deposit account. Merchants like one device that does everything.

GCash noticed this and has started rolling out its own card readers and QR soundboxes for small businesses, which breaks Maya’s absolute monopoly on the counter. But for the big chains, Maya is still king. It spent over a decade laying those pipes, one store at a time. That’s very hard to copy, and it’s why Maya says it handles the majority of card payments in the country.

And remember the maturity gap. GCash has been around since 2004 and hit hyper-growth during the 2020 pandemic. Maya only launched its digital bank in 2022. Many digital banks burn investor money for five to seven years before they break even. Maya did it in about three.

When is Maya going public?

Which brings us to the big question. If GCash is already public, when is Maya’s turn? Originally, PLDT floated listing Maya in both the US and the Philippines by the second half of 2026. That didn’t happen; Pangilinan has confirmed the timeline has slipped to 2027.

There are a few reasons. One is a standoff between shareholders: KKR, the private equity giant, owns about 30% of Maya and wants a big, high-valuation exit, ideally on a US exchange, while PLDT wants to protect its control without overpaying for it. But the reason that sounds best in a press release is this: the Philippine market needs time to absorb GCash first. Dropping two massive fintech IPOs back to back could drain the market. So Maya waits. In other words, let GCash test the waters.

Did waiting ever work? Ask Uber and Lyft

We’ve seen this play out before: Lyft versus Uber, 2019. The two raced each other to go public. Lyft went first in March 2019, priced at $72, jumped over 20% on day one, and everyone celebrated. Then within days it sank below its IPO price.

Uber watched all of that happen. So when it listed two months later, it played it safe: $45, the low end of its range, at a valuation around $82 billion, way below the $120 billion bankers had floated the year before. And even after all that caution, Uber still dropped about 7.6% on day one, one of the biggest first-day dollar losses in US IPO history. So did going second save Uber? Not really. Lyft’s stumble just made Uber cheaper. But years later, Uber turned profitable and its stock climbed well above its IPO price, while Lyft stayed the smaller player. The timing didn’t decide who won. The business did.

Now apply that to Maya, with a twist: in the Uber and Lyft story, the smaller company went first. Here, the giant went first. And GCash’s debut was quiet. No first-day pop, and a price well below the ₱10 ceiling it was reportedly aiming for. That just set the price tag for Philippine fintech. Do the quick math: GCash is valued at about ₱442 billion on ₱17 billion of profit, roughly 25 times earnings. Apply that same measuring stick to Maya’s ₱1.7 billion profit and you get nowhere near the number KKR wants.

So here’s my take. Maya’s delay probably isn’t just about being polite to the market. Maya needs another year or two of profit growth before it can walk into an IPO and justify a premium. And honestly? If its banking engine keeps compounding the way it has, that’s a very reasonable bet.

So who’s winning?

GCash, by a mile, for now. But it’s really two different games. GCash is winning the attention game: users, habits, the word on everyone’s lips. Maya is playing the margin game: deposits, loans, and the terminals at the counter. GCash has the bigger empire today, but Maya has quietly built a stronger engine, with almost a two-decade head start to make up. In 2027, when Maya finally rings that same bell, we’ll find out which game the market actually cares about.

GCash vs Maya FAQ

How many users does GCash have compared to Maya?

GCash has over 94 million registered accounts and 41.5 million monthly active users, and processed about ₱17 trillion in payment transactions in 2025. Maya's digital banking app has around 10.7 million bank customers, while processing over ₱1 trillion in merchant payments as one of the country's top business acquirers.

Is Maya profitable?

Yes. Maya booked its first full year of profit in 2025 with ₱1.7 billion in net income, about three years after launching its digital bank in 2022. That is exceptionally fast; many digital banks burn investor money for five to seven years before breaking even. It is still roughly a tenth of what GCash parent Mynt made the same year.

When is Maya's IPO?

PLDT originally floated a dual US and Philippine listing by the second half of 2026, but chairman Manny Pangilinan has confirmed the timeline slipped to 2027. The delay involves a shareholder standoff with KKR, which owns about 30% of Maya and wants a high-valuation exit, plus giving the market time to absorb GCash's massive float first.

Does Maya earn money when people pay with GCash?

Often, yes. Maya is the primary payment acquirer for major chains like McDonald's, Starbucks, Jollibee, and Mercury Drug, so payments at those counters frequently flow through a Maya terminal whether the customer uses a card, a QR code, Apple Pay, or GCash. When a GCash wallet is used on a Maya terminal, Maya takes a processing cut.
