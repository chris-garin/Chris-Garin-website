Why Japan Surplus Shops Are Everywhere in the Philippines

Chris Garin • November 18, 2026 • 4 min read

The short answer

“Japan surplus” isn’t extra stock from a factory. It’s used goods from Japanese homes. In Japan, throwing away furniture, appliances, and bikes costs money: you buy a garbage sticker for a sofa, and by law you pay a recycling fee for a TV, ref, or aircon that can run over ₱2,000. So people hand working items to recycle shops and free collectors instead. Those get packed into shipping containers, auctioned off, and sold here, where what Japan treats as a garbage problem, Filipinos treat as a bargain.

There’s a booming Japan surplus industry in the Philippines, and it exists because of a simple mismatch: what we see as a bargain, Japan sees as a garbage problem. Here’s how it all works.

Why does throwing things away cost money in Japan?

You know how some eat-all-you-can restaurants tell you, “If you have leftovers, we’ll charge you”? Japan works kind of like that with furniture, appliances, and bikes. Getting rid of them isn’t free.

Want to dispose of an old sofa? You have to buy a special garbage sticker first, before the city will pick it up. And for appliances it goes further: by law, you pay a recycling fee just to get rid of an old TV, refrigerator, or aircon. For a ref, that fee can run over two thousand pesos. You pay money to make your own refrigerator disappear.

So where does “Japan surplus” actually come from?

Faced with paying to throw something away, people do the obvious thing: they sell it, or give it away. Working items go to recycle shops, which is what Japan calls its secondhand stores, or to collectors in small trucks who drive around neighborhoods offering to haul things off for free.

Those collectors and shops sort the items, pack them into shipping containers along with thousands of other items, and sell or auction off the containers. That’s what “Japan surplus” really is. Not factory overstock, not excess inventory. Used stuff from Japanese homes, rerouted from the disposal system into an export business.

Why does Japan have so much good secondhand stuff?

Three reasons keep the stream flowing. First, Japanese households upgrade often, so perfectly good items get replaced on a regular cycle. Second, apartments are small. There’s nowhere to hoard the old sofa when the new one arrives, so things have to go. And third, for a long time, buying secondhand in Japan carried a stigma, which meant the local market for used goods was thin.

Put those together and you get a steady supply of items that are well kept, still working, and unwanted where they are. The quality isn’t an accident. It’s the product of a culture that maintains its things and a system that makes keeping or dumping them expensive.

How do surplus shops make money on a mystery box?

Importing by container is basically buying a giant mystery box. The importer doesn’t hand-pick each lamp and bike; they buy the whole container, which means they pay very little per item.

Sure, some of what’s inside is junk. But the good stuff more than pays for the whole container, and by the time an item reaches a shop here, it sells at a nice profit. Cheap intake, decent sell-through, and a supply that never dries up, because Japan keeps producing unwanted goods every day. That’s the whole engine, and it’s why new surplus shops keep opening across the Philippines.

Japan Surplus FAQ

Is Japan surplus brand new?

No. Despite the name, Japan surplus is not extra stock from factories. It is secondhand goods from Japanese homes: furniture, appliances, bikes, and housewares that owners replaced or no longer wanted. Much of it is well kept and still working, because disposing of items in Japan costs money and households maintain their things.

Why do Japanese people give away working appliances?

Because throwing them away costs money. Bulky items like sofas need a paid garbage sticker before the city collects them, and by law appliances like TVs, refrigerators, and aircons carry a recycling fee that can run over ₱2,000 for a ref. Selling to a recycle shop or handing items to a free collector avoids the fee entirely.

How does Japan surplus get to the Philippines?

Collectors and recycle shops in Japan sort the items they gather, pack thousands of them into shipping containers, and sell or auction off the containers. Philippine importers buy by the container, which keeps the cost per item very low, then distribute the contents to local surplus shops.

Is a Japan surplus shop a good business?

The economics favor it. Buying by the container means paying very little per item, and while some of each load is junk, the good items more than pay for the whole container. With resale margins on top and a supply stream that never dries up, the model keeps attracting new entrants, which is why surplus shops keep opening.
