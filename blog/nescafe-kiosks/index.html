Why Nescafé Suddenly Opened ₱30 Cafés

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 6 min read

The short answer

Nescafé’s ₱30 kiosks are not an attack on small coffee shops. They are Nescafé guarding its own customers. Budget chains like Pickup Coffee reset what Filipinos think a daily cup should cost, which means sachet drinkers can now afford to buy coffee outside the house. Nescafé is in the daily coffee business, not the instant powder business, so instead of watching that habit walk away, it followed its customers out the door with a ₱30 cup from a brand they already trust.

For decades, Nescafé won the Filipino morning without ever selling you a single cup. It sold you a stick, a sachet, the 3-in-1 you made at home. So why is the brand that already owns your kitchen suddenly opening ₱30 coffee stands?

Is Nescafé attacking small coffee shops?

That’s what most people assume, and the kiosks have sparked plenty of online debate (especially on Threads) about whether small, independent shops can survive a multinational selling drinks for as low as ₱30. But the move makes a lot more sense read the other way: Nescafé is defending its own customers before someone else takes them.

To see why, you need a concept from 1960.

What do American railroads have to do with coffee?

In 1960, Harvard professor Theodore Levitt coined the term Marketing Myopia to explain why America’s railroad companies fell into decline. For decades they were some of the most profitable businesses in the country, because trains were how America moved, and no train could go anywhere without their tracks.

Then came cars, buses, and highways. Little by little, fewer people needed trains. Passenger numbers kept falling, and many railroad companies slowly went bankrupt.

But here’s the thing: people didn’t stop traveling. They traveled more than ever. The railroad companies just thought they were in the railroad business, when they were really in the transportation business. So instead of following their customers into roads, buses, or trucking, they kept laying railroads and watched those customers leave.

What business is Nescafé really in?

On the surface, Nescafé looks like it’s in the instant coffee business. It’s actually in the daily coffee business. It wants to be the coffee you drink regardless of who you are and where you are. If it defines itself by the sachet, it shrinks with the sachet. If it defines itself by the daily coffee moment, it follows that moment wherever it goes.

That’s why Nestlé built a product for every type of coffee drinker. Simple, cheap coffee at home? The stick and the 3-in-1. Graduated from the stick but still brewing at home? Nescafé Gold. Café-tasting coffee without leaving the house? Nespresso. And when specialty coffee took off, Nestlé bought a majority stake in Blue Bottle Coffee, one of America’s best-known third-wave cafés.

A product for almost every way you drink coffee at home. But then a new trend emerged outside of home.

How did Pickup Coffee change the math?

For years, Filipinos had two options. Make cheap coffee at home with your Nescafé stick, or pay ₱180 to ₱250 at a café. The responsible thing was to stir up coffee at home so you didn’t have to spend that much outside.

Then Pickup Coffee, launched in 2022, showed everyone that café-style coffee could cost around ₱100. Suddenly you’re thinking: hey, this isn’t so bad. I’d still save by making coffee at home, but at ₱100, I could do this daily. Then smaller shops went even lower: ₱50, ₱40 coffee.

That unlocked a new kind of buyer. Failing to prepare your instant coffee at home no longer wrecked your daily budget, because the alternative was no longer a ₱200 café drink. And that created the real threat: for the first time, people were willing to let go of their cheap Nescafé sticks.

Nescafé wasn’t about to watch them leave the way the railroads did. So it followed its customers out the door, with ₱30 coffee to go from a brand they already trust.

How do Nescafé kiosks compare with Pickup Coffee?

They’re playing different games. Nescafé kiosks run from about ₱30 to ₱75 (a Spanish Latte sits around ₱50), built on soluble coffee mixed with milk, creamer, and syrups, and placed in residential communities and neighborhood commercial areas. Pickup runs about ₱50 to ₱130 (its Kape Kastila around ₱75 to ₱80), pulls real espresso from freshly ground beans, and plants itself in malls, business districts, and high-traffic zones.

The kiosk’s price point is also clever psychology. The jump from a ₱10 sachet to a ₱150 latte is huge; ₱30 sits right in that missing middle, small enough to try without thinking, small enough to fit the daily baon budget instead of the treat budget. And reviewers of the Tondo kiosk noted the taste is close to familiar home coffee, which isn’t a weakness. It’s the point: graduating from the sachet feels safe when the cup tastes like the brand you grew up with. Placing stands near markets, groceries, and commuter routes in places like Tondo and Sampaloc puts Nescafé exactly where the new habit forms.

Who should actually worry?

According to industry observers, specialty and premium cafés selling at ₱120 to ₱200 have little reason to panic. They serve a different market that values freshly brewed beans and the sit-down experience. The pressure falls on “pseudo-cafés”: budget shops selling sweet, syrupy, powder-based drinks at much higher prices than a ₱30 kiosk.

The deeper disruption isn’t any single competitor going out of business. It’s that Nescafé is shifting what Filipinos consider a fair, reasonable price for an everyday cup of coffee.

The lesson: cannibalize yourself first

Steve Jobs pushed the iPhone knowing it would kill the iPod. Netflix moved to streaming while its DVD business was still profitable. Nescafé’s kiosks may eat a bit of sachet volume, but losing a sachet sale to your own kiosk beats losing that customer to Pickup forever.

Will demand for instant coffee actually shrink? Honestly, there’s no hard public data on that. But if Nescafé is investing this heavily, it probably knows something we don’t. Nescafé knows there may come a point when you finally let go of the stick. It just wants to make sure that when you do, you’ll still be drinking Nescafé.

Nescafé kiosks FAQ

Why is Nescafé opening its own coffee kiosks?

Because the habit is shifting. Budget chains like Pickup Coffee made buying coffee outside affordable, so sachet drinkers are starting to buy their daily cup instead of making it at home. Nescafé sees itself as being in the daily coffee business, not just instant powder, so the kiosks follow its customers into the new habit before another brand captures them.

How much does coffee cost at a Nescafé kiosk?

Drinks run from about ₱30 to ₱75, with a Spanish Latte at around ₱50. That compares with roughly ₱50 to ₱130 at Pickup Coffee and ₱120 to ₱200 at specialty and premium cafés.

Will Nescafé kiosks kill small coffee shops?

Specialty and premium cafés serve a different market that values fresh espresso and the sit-down experience, so they have little reason to worry. The pressure falls on budget “pseudo-cafés” selling sweet, powder-based drinks at higher prices. The bigger effect is psychological: the kiosks are resetting what Filipinos consider a fair price for everyday coffee.

Is Nescafé kiosk coffee made from instant coffee?

Yes. The kiosk drinks are built on soluble coffee mixed with milk, creamer, and syrups, unlike Pickup Coffee’s espresso from freshly ground beans. Reviewers note the taste is close to familiar home coffee, which is deliberate: it makes the step up from the sachet feel safe.
