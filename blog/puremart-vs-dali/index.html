Puremart vs DALI: Puregold’s Fighter Brand Strategy, Explained

Chris Garin • November 17, 2026 • 5 min read

The short answer

Instead of cutting its own prices to fight DALI, Puregold launched a separate small-format chain, Puremart, and sent it to the front line. That’s a classic fighter brand strategy: the main brand doesn’t change a thing while the fighter absorbs the price war. Puremart matched DALI’s prices on around 120 everyday essentials like rice, eggs, sugar, and cooking oil, betting on the halo effect: make the basics cheap and shoppers assume the whole store is cheap, so they never get a reason to switch.

The discount wars are on. Puregold is fighting back against DALI, and it’s a genuinely clever strategy. Not a price cut. Not a rebrand. A whole new store.

What is a fighter brand strategy?

Imagine you run a Filipino restaurant. Better ingredients, a nice dining area, meals around ₱250. Then a carinderia opens right next door selling similar dishes for around ₱80. Your food is obviously better. But at that price, theirs is good enough for a lot of people, and you notice some of your regulars have started eating there instead.

So how do you get them back? You could cut your prices. But then you’d make less on every meal, and your restaurant starts looking like just another cheap place to eat. The brand you spent years building gets dragged down with the discount.

So instead of changing your restaurant, you open your own carinderia down the street, right beside theirs, and let it do the fighting. Your restaurant doesn’t change a thing. Same food, same prices, same reason your customers loved it. Now it’s carinderia versus carinderia. That’s the fighter brand strategy, and it’s exactly what Puregold is running against DALI.

Why is DALI a threat to Puregold?

Puregold is the big supermarket you drive to for your weekly or monthly grocery run. DALI flipped that model: it’s a small store that comes to you. Because each branch is roughly convenience-store size, DALI can open right on busy neighborhood corners where people walk past every day.

Then there’s the hard-discount playbook. Instead of stocking the Datu Puti or UFC you’re used to, DALI makes its own house-brand versions of vinegar, banana ketchup, and the other staples on your list. Add the rest of the tricks, like spending nothing on ads, running each store with the bare minimum of staff, and putting products on the shelf still in their shipping boxes, and DALI can sell for less, usually undercutting Puregold’s prices.

Small stores, closer to home, cheaper basics. That combination quietly pulls the everyday top-up trips, and eventually the habit, away from the big supermarket.

How does Puremart fight back?

Puremart is Puregold’s answer: a store roughly the same size as DALI, planted in the same kind of neighborhood locations. But here’s the clever part. Puremart isn’t trying to undercut DALI. It’s matching it.

The tricky thing is that Puremart sells the big brands we’re familiar with: Purefoods, Tender Juicy, Nescafé. It can’t slash those prices, and they’ll almost always cost more than DALI’s house brands. So Puremart made a different bet. People have been buying those branded products at those prices forever anyway. Leave them alone. Instead, go hard on the everyday basics: rice, eggs, sugar, cooking oil. The things every household needs all the time.

So that’s what it did. Puremart matched DALI’s prices on around 120 essential items that DALI sells too.

What is the halo effect, and why does matching work?

Those 120 items are doing two jobs. The first is psychological. When a store makes the products you buy most often really cheap, you tend to assume everything else there is cheap too. That’s the halo effect. The cheap rice and cooking oil cast a “this whole store is mura” glow over every shelf, including the branded goods that were never discounted. You walk in for the basics, and right there on the shelf are the brands you already trust, at the prices you were always willing to pay.

The second job is defensive. Puremart’s real goal isn’t to beat DALI on price. It’s to stop you from even considering the switch. People don’t change stores without a reason, and if the prices on the things that matter are the same, what’s the point? Even when DALI is slightly cheaper, the gap is too small to notice. Most people only switch when the difference is big enough to feel. Close that gap, and habit takes over. The store you already know, the brand you already trust, wins by default.

Who wins the discount wars?

That’s the elegance of the whole play. Puregold proper never had to touch its prices or its positioning. The fighter brand absorbs the war while the main brand stays premium, exactly like the restaurant that opened its own carinderia instead of discounting its menu.

DALI still has structural advantages: its house brands give it margin room that branded goods never will, and its no-frills operation keeps costs brutally low. But Puremart doesn’t need to win every shelf. It only needs to make switching feel pointless. In a fight between two corner stores selling the same cheap rice, the one backed by the brands Filipinos already know has a very real edge.

Puremart vs DALI FAQ

What is Puremart?

Puremart is Puregold's small-format store built to compete with DALI. Each branch is roughly the size of a DALI store and sits in the same kind of busy neighborhood locations. Unlike DALI, it sells the big brands Filipinos already know, like Purefoods, Tender Juicy, and Nescafé, while matching DALI's prices on around 120 everyday essentials.

What is a fighter brand strategy?

A fighter brand is a separate, cheaper brand a company launches specifically to fight a low-cost competitor, so the main brand never has to cut its own prices or dilute its positioning. The main brand stays untouched while the fighter absorbs the price war. Puremart is Puregold's fighter brand against DALI.

Why is DALI so cheap?

DALI runs a hard-discount model. It replaces familiar brands with its own house-brand equivalents, spends essentially nothing on advertising, runs each store with minimal staff, and displays products on shelves still in their shipping boxes. Small convenience-store-sized branches on busy corners keep costs down and put it closer to shoppers than a big supermarket.

Why does Puremart match DALI's prices instead of undercutting them?

Because matching is enough. People only switch stores when the price gap is big enough to notice, so Puremart's goal is simply to erase any reason to switch. Matching around 120 essentials also triggers the halo effect: when the basics are cheap, shoppers assume the whole store is cheap, then buy the branded goods at regular prices once they're inside.
