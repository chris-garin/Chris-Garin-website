The “German” Brands You Can Only Buy at S&R

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 6 min read

The short answer

Blakk and Kuchenluxe look like imported European appliance brands, but you can’t buy them anywhere except S&R. They’re effectively S&R’s house brands: white-label appliances sourced from contract factories, mostly in China, and given new names. The German-sounding branding borrows prestige through the country-of-origin effect, the low prices make your membership card feel worth renewing, and the separate brand names protect S&R’s own name if anything goes wrong.

Have you seen those foreign-looking appliance brands in S&R? Kuchenluxe. Blakk. Brands we’ve never heard of. Brands I always just assumed were big in some European country. I looked them up expecting some old German company. Turns out, you can’t buy them anywhere except S&R. And there’s an entire playbook behind it.

I made a short video about this too, if you’d rather watch the quick version first.

Who actually owns Blakk and Kuchenluxe?

Blakk and Kuchenluxe are basically S&R’s house brands, and they usually cost much less than the bigger, more popular brands sitting right next to them. Blakk is the classic white label: S&R sources generic appliance designs straight from major contract factories, the factory changes nothing about the machine, and the Blakk logo goes on the chassis and the box. Kuchenluxe works the same way with a marketing twist: it’s an exclusively distributed brand curated for S&R floors, marketed with a “German-designed” aesthetic, but built in the same third-party factories that budget brands use.

There’s a related tier on those same shelves: Tylr and Baumann Living, two Hong Kong-based brands “inspired by German sensibilities,” managed by T3 Integrated Business Solutions, a Hong Kong company. Different ownership, same playbook: European-feeling names on mass-market appliances.

Why does S&R sell its own appliance brands?

To get it, you have to understand how S&R makes money. It’s not just what you buy. It’s your membership card. Every year, you pay just to get in. So S&R’s whole job is to keep proving that card is worth renewing.

And nothing proves it faster than finding something good that’s cheaper than the famous brands, and that you can only find in S&R. Here’s the trick: it doesn’t have to be everything. When enough items feel like a steal, your brain assumes the whole store is cheap. It’s a halo effect. A handful of well-placed deals can make an entire warehouse feel like a bargain.

Appliances make great anchors for this. Put a premium brand air fryer on the shelf for ₱12,000, then put a Blakk or Kuchenluxe model with the same basic features right next to it for ₱4,500. The premium brand tells your brain a machine doing this job should cost ₱12,000. Suddenly the house brand feels like an absolute steal. The appliances don’t need fancy tech. They just have to work, and be cheaper.

What is white labeling, and who else does it?

Best part? S&R didn’t even have to make these products. It’s called white labeling, or OEM. Giant factories, many of them in China, already build microwaves and air fryers for dozens of brands around the world. S&R, or a partner working with S&R, picks an air fryer straight from the factory’s catalog, puts a new name on it, and ships it here. No famous brand charging extra for its name. No local distributor taking a cut. No big ad campaigns to pay for. That’s how a Blakk air fryer ends up cheaper than the big name beside it.

This isn’t an S&R invention. In the US, private and white label retail is a $280 billion-plus industry and a primary growth engine for the biggest chains. Amazon Basics is white labeling. Costco’s Kirkland Signature is the premium version, where name brands quietly do the manufacturing: Starbucks roasts Kirkland coffee, Duracell makes Kirkland batteries, and Reynolds produces Kirkland aluminum foil. S&R is running the warehouse-club playbook, localized.

It also lands because brand loyalty is dying. Shoppers squeezed by inflation no longer care whose name is on the box; they care whether the machine works for the price. A decent white-label appliance inside a members-only store makes you feel like a smart shopper, not someone settling for the cheap option.

Why not just put the S&R logo on them?

Three reasons. First, risk. Appliances break. Sometimes they overheat. If an S&R air fryer goes viral for catching fire, that’s S&R’s name in the headlines. If it’s a Blakk air fryer? That’s a Blakk problem.

Second, perception. An appliance with the S&R logo would feel like a generic budget version sitting beside Panasonic or Philips. But Blakk looks like just another brand on the shelf, going head to head with the big names. Same rice cooker. Completely different first impression.

Third, foreign branding. German-made products are seen as higher quality, so a German-sounding name lets a brand borrow that reputation for free. Before you even check the price tag, “Kuchenluxe” has already convinced you it’s well-made. “S&R” could never do that.

Why do German-sounding names work on us?

The mechanism is the country-of-origin effect: people judge a product partly by where they think it comes from, because certain countries are believed to make certain things better. People trust Japan’s home appliances, Korea’s skincare, Italian fashion. The Philippines is full of this. Argentina Corned Beef is not from Argentina. Japan Home Centre is from Hong Kong. Giordano sounds Italian, but it’s also from Hong Kong. American Home isn’t from America, and Fujidenzo, Hanabishi, and Asahi are all Filipino, not Japanese. Even the giants play: Häagen-Dazs is from the Bronx.

The fake nationality is even a timestamp. Brands borrow whichever country owns their category at the moment they’re born. Filipino fan and rice cooker brands of the 1980s went Japanese, because Japan owned electronics then. Chinese skincare brands today go Korean, because Korea now owns beauty.

None of these brands is lying, exactly. Hanabishi never claimed it was Japanese. Call it borrowed prestige. The line gets crossed when a brand fakes its passport outright: Mumuso added a .kr to its website, printed Korean-looking symbols on its packaging that turned out to be gibberish, claimed it came from Korea, and even opened a Seoul office with no employees. Miniso mimicked Muji’s name and aesthetic, Daiso’s inventory style, and Uniqlo’s red square logo, and publicly apologized in 2022. Borrowing prestige is fair game. Faking your passport is where branding becomes lying.

So is it a scam?

No. Plenty of these appliances are solid. They’re just not from some old German company. They’re from S&R. And the next time you grab one and feel like you just scored the deal of the year, remember: that feeling was the whole plan.

S&R house brands FAQ

Who owns Blakk and Kuchenluxe?

They’re effectively S&R house brands, sold only at S&R. Blakk is a white-label line sourced directly from contract appliance factories, while Kuchenluxe is an exclusively distributed brand curated for S&R with a German-designed aesthetic, built in the same third-party factories budget brands use.

Is Kuchenluxe a German brand?

No. Kuchenluxe is marketed with a German-sounding name and a European feel, but the manufacturing relies on standard mass-market OEM factories, mostly in China. The name works through foreign branding: German-made products are seen as higher quality, so the name borrows that reputation before you even check the price tag.

Are Blakk and Kuchenluxe appliances any good?

Plenty of them are solid. They use standard mass-market designs and components from the same factories that build appliances for brands worldwide. They’re cheaper mainly because there’s no famous brand premium, no local distributor’s cut, and no big ad campaigns baked into the price.

Why doesn’t S&R just use its own name on these products?

Three reasons: risk, because a defective product under a separate brand doesn’t drag S&R’s name into the headlines; perception, because an S&R-branded appliance would feel generic next to Panasonic or Philips while Blakk reads as a real competing brand; and foreign branding, because a European-sounding name signals quality in a way the store’s own name can’t.
