Starbucks Made Wasting Money a Flex

Chris Garin • November 16, 2026 • 5 min read

The short answer

The Starbucks planner promo gets you to commit to eighteen purchases, roughly ₱2,800 to ₱3,000 in drinks, during the exact season you have extra cash. The sticker card runs on the goal-gradient effect: the closer you get to a goal, the harder you push to finish it. And the planner itself works as conspicuous waste: buying something unnecessary at a premium is precisely what makes it a status signal. It was never really about the tumbler.

Ever tell a friend, “You know, instead of collecting all those stickers, you could just buy an Owala or a Hydro Flask for the same amount, probably even less”? Has that ever worked? Of course not. Because it was never really about the tumbler.

How does the 18-sticker mechanic hook you?

The “collect 18 stickers” mechanic is one of the most genius marketing campaigns ever. While every other brand runs one-time sales promos during the Christmas season, when everyone has extra cash from 13th month pay and bonuses, Starbucks gets you to commit to eighteen purchases. Not one coffee. Eighteen.

And you didn’t even ask to sign up. Maybe you couldn’t care less about the tumbler or the planner. You’re just there for coffee, and the barista goes, “Would you like to collect stickers? It’s free,” and you’re like, “Sure, whatever.” Now you have one sticker. But you don’t care. You only come here for the delicious, definitely-not-burnt hot Americano anyway.

Then you realize you have seven stickers already. Just eleven more to go. You still think you don’t care, but you’re starting to pick Starbucks over the café next door, and you find yourself doing sticker math, figuring out how many more you need to get the tumbler.

What is the goal-gradient effect?

All of this was carefully orchestrated. Starbucks took notes from a concept called the goal-gradient effect: the closer you get to a goal, the harder you push to finish it. Researchers at Columbia actually tested this with a café reward card, and people bought coffee more often the closer they got to the free drink. Same coffee, same price. The only thing that changed was how close they were to the finish line.

The sunk cost fallacy backs it up. Once you have five or ten stickers, your brain says, “I’ve already spent this much. I might as well keep going so those stickers don’t go to waste.” The invested effort locks you into further spending. The eighteen stickers translate to roughly ₱2,800 or more spent on drinks, and the card turns all of it into a game.

Why buy it when cheaper tumblers exist?

If you think people should just buy a better tumbler online because it’ll be cheaper, and that this whole thing is unnecessary, you’re missing the whole point. Because “unnecessary” is exactly why people want it.

It’s called conspicuous waste. An economist named Thorstein Veblen came up with it over a hundred years ago. He noticed that rich people didn’t just show off by buying expensive things. They showed off by wasting money on things they clearly didn’t need. Like a big, perfectly trimmed front lawn. That’s land you could farm, but you don’t, because you don’t have to. The less practical the purchase, the louder the signal.

Years ago, a Starbucks cup said, “He must be rich. He can afford expensive coffee.” But Starbucks isn’t the premium option anymore. There are local cafes everywhere serving better coffee, many of them for the same price or less. So the message changed to: “He must be rich, because even with cheaper options around, he just doesn’t care.” The flex isn’t the price anymore. It’s being able to make an unoptimized choice and not think twice about it.

In consumer psychology, this is badge value. Some products work like a badge: you carry them around, and they tell people about your lifestyle without you saying a word. The planner says you had the disposable income for eighteen handcrafted drinks in a few weeks. For many people it’s also a ritual: each sticker is a small break from a stressful workday, and the final item is a trophy for surviving another year. Which explains the strangest stat of all: a huge share of people who complete the stickers never actually use the planner. They’re not paying for pages. They’re paying for the chase and what the object says about them.

What does Starbucks get out of it?

Think about it from Starbucks’ side. Normally, a customer buys one coffee and maybe comes back, maybe not. With the planner, every person who claims one has made eighteen purchases. That’s eighteen guaranteed visits, around three thousand pesos per customer, right in the holiday season, the highest-revenue stretch of the year. In marketing terms, that’s customer lifetime value, and the sticker card locks in a chunk of it upfront.

The promo also stacks several named tactics at once. The planner is a limited-time offering, available only during the holidays, which triggers fear of missing out. The card is a classic frequency program, like airline miles compressed into one intense season. And in recent years the campaign moved primarily to the Starbucks Rewards app: you track stickers digitally, preload money, which softens the pain of paying, and hand Starbucks data it can use to target you with offers for the rest of the year.

So no, telling your friend the math doesn’t work. The math was never the point. Starbucks isn’t selling you a tumbler. It’s selling you something that tells the world you didn’t need a cheaper one.

Starbucks planner FAQ

How much do you spend to get a Starbucks planner?

You need 18 stickers, which works out to roughly ₱2,800 to ₱3,000 spent on drinks, depending on what you order. The promo runs from late October through January, timed to the holiday season when 13th month pay and bonuses leave people with extra cash.

What is the goal-gradient effect?

It’s the principle that people push harder the closer they get to a goal. Researchers at Columbia tested it with a café reward card: customers bought coffee more often as they neared the free drink, even though the coffee and the price never changed. The 18-sticker card applies the same mechanic to the planner.

Why do people buy the planner when cheaper tumblers exist?

Because the waste is the point. Thorstein Veblen called it conspicuous waste: spending on something clearly unnecessary signals that you can afford not to care. With cheaper and often better alternatives everywhere, choosing Starbucks anyway became the flex. The planner works as a badge that broadcasts disposable income without a word.

Do people actually use the Starbucks planner?

A huge share of collectors never use it. The planner functions as a trophy for completing the chase and a status object, not a productivity tool. For many it also marks a ritual: each sticker was a small reward during a stressful season, and the finished planner is proof of it.
